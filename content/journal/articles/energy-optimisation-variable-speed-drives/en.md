# Energy Optimisation Using Variable-Speed Drives

## Executive Summary

Variable-speed energy proposals are usually built on a single number: a percentage saving, applied to a nameplate rating, over an assumed number of operating hours. Almost every disappointing project began with exactly that calculation, and almost every successful one began by discarding it.

**The saving does not live in the drive. It lives in the difference between the energy the machine currently consumes across its real duty profile and the energy the same duty would require at variable speed.** Where the machine spends most of its life at full output, that difference can be negative, because the drive's own losses apply whether or not it is slowing anything down.

This article is about establishing that difference honestly — the physics that creates the saving, the four mechanisms that erode it, and the measurement discipline that separates a real result from a modelled one.

## Where the Saving Actually Comes From

A fixed-speed centrifugal machine controlled by throttling produces more head than the process needs and then destroys the excess across a valve or damper. The motor still delivers the power; the valve converts the surplus into turbulence and heat.

Variable speed removes the surplus at the source: the impeller does less work rather than doing full work into a restriction.

For a centrifugal machine, the scaling relationships are:

```text
Q2 / Q1 = N2 / N1              flow scales with speed
H2 / H1 = (N2 / N1)^2          head scales with speed squared
P2 / P1 = (N2 / N1)^3          shaft power scales with speed cubed

  Q = flow, H = head, N = rotational speed, P = shaft power
  Subscript 1 = reference condition, 2 = new condition

Assumptions and limits:
  - applies to the MACHINE (pump or fan), not to the installed system
  - assumes geometric similarity and constant efficiency between the
    two conditions, which is an approximation that weakens as speed falls
  - the operating point is set by where the machine curve meets the
    SYSTEM curve; the cube relationship describes the machine, and only
    describes the installation when the system curve passes through the origin
```

**That last line is the single most important qualification in this field**, and it is the one that disappears from most proposals.

## The Four Things That Erode the Saving

### 1. Static head

A system curve passes through the origin only when every component of the resistance is generated by flow. Elevation and vessel back pressure are not: they are present at zero flow and remain present at every speed, so that portion of the duty cannot be reduced by slowing the machine down.

```text
H_system = H_static + H_friction(Q)

  H_static   = fixed component: elevation, vessel pressure, back pressure
  H_friction = flow-dependent component, rising with flow
```

Two consequences follow directly:

- **The cube law over-predicts the saving**, sometimes by a large margin, because the machine must continue to produce the static head no matter how little it is delivering.
- **There is a minimum useful speed** below which the machine produces less head than the static requirement and flow stops entirely. Below the speed at which the static component alone consumes the whole developed head, the machine delivers nothing at all, and that speed — not the drive's minimum — is the floor the savings calculation has to use.

**A high-static system is not a poor candidate for variable speed — but its saving must be computed from its own system curve, and it will not resemble the cube law.**

### 2. Machine efficiency away from the design point

The affinity relationships assume constant efficiency, which is an approximation. As a pump moves away from its best efficiency point — which reduced speed on a static-head system will do — its efficiency falls, partially offsetting the reduction in hydraulic power. A model that applies the cube law to shaft power and ignores the efficiency shift will over-state the result.

### 3. Minimum-speed constraints imposed by the process

These are hard limits, they come from process engineering rather than electrical engineering, and they routinely remove the bottom of the speed range that the saving calculation assumed:

- **Minimum flow through a pump** to prevent recirculation and thermal damage.
- **Minimum velocity in a pipeline** to keep solids in suspension, or in a duct to keep dust entrained.
- **Minimum air flow** for combustion, drying, cooling, or ventilation compliance.
- **Minimum agitation** for mixing or heat transfer.
- **Minimum cooling airflow** for the driven equipment or the motor itself.

**Any of these can make the calculated saving unreachable**, and none of them appear in an electrical model. They are found by asking the process engineer, not by inspecting the pump curve.

### 4. Losses added by the conversion

The drive and the motor both introduce or shift losses:

- **The drive dissipates a fraction of throughput continuously**, and that loss applies at every speed including full speed. It is a permanent subtraction from the benefit.
- **The motor is slightly less efficient on a drive supply** than on a sinusoidal supply, because of the additional harmonic content in the motor current.
- **Motor efficiency falls at reduced load**, so a machine that spends its life lightly loaded is running its motor in a less efficient region regardless of the drive.
- **Cooling load rises** where the drive's heat is added to an electrical room that is air-conditioned; part of the saved energy is spent removing the heat of the device that saved it.

**The honest consequence of this section: a machine that runs at or near full output most of the time will not save energy on a drive, and may consume slightly more.** The drive may still be justified — for process control, soft starting, or protection — but not on an energy argument.

## The Duty Profile Is the Whole Calculation

The correct method is unremarkable and rarely followed.

**Measure how the machine actually runs.** Not the design case, not the nameplate, and not the operator's recollection. Log flow or pressure, and the motor's electrical input, over a period that covers the real variation — shifts, product changes, day and night, and the seasonal extremes if they matter.

Then build the profile:

| Operating band | Hours per year in that band | Present input power | Input power at variable speed | Difference |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Full output | … | measured | measured or modelled | usually negative |
| Part output, high | … | measured | modelled from system curve | positive |
| Part output, low | … | measured | modelled from system curve | strongly positive |
| Off | … | 0 | 0 | 0 |

**The saving is the sum over that table, not the value in any one row.** A machine with a wide operating range and long hours at part load is an excellent candidate. A machine at full output for eight thousand hours a year is not, regardless of its size.

**A useful screening question before any modelling: how far open is the throttling valve, and for how much of the year?** A valve that is nearly fully open most of the time is telling you that there is very little surplus energy to recover.

For the electrical measurement itself:

```text
P = √3 × V × I × PF

  P  = three-phase active power (W)
  V  = line-to-line voltage (V)
  I  = line current (A)
  PF = power factor, defined as P / S (true power factor)

Assumptions and limits:
  - balanced three-phase system
  - PF here must be the TRUE power factor, not the displacement factor;
    with distorting loads the two differ and using the displacement value
    will overstate the real power
  - for a drive input, distortion is significant, so an instrument that
    measures true power directly is preferable to this calculation
```

## Oversizing: The Cheaper Fix That Gets Skipped

The installations where a drive appears to deliver spectacular savings are very often installations with an oversized machine, throttled hard to compensate. The drive genuinely reduces consumption — and it does so by managing the consequences of a sizing error rather than correcting it.

Before specifying a drive, the alternatives deserve a fair comparison:

- **Trimming or replacing the impeller** to match the actual duty. Inexpensive, permanent, no added complexity, no electronics in the running path.
- **Replacing the machine** with a correctly sized one where the mismatch is severe, which also brings a modern efficiency class and often a smaller motor.
- **Changing the control philosophy** — for instance, sequencing two machines instead of throttling one, or eliminating an unnecessary pressure requirement upstream.

**The comparison to make honestly: an oversized machine driven at reduced speed still carries the losses of an oversized motor**, and the drive has to run at partial speed permanently to compensate for something a mechanical change would have fixed once.

Where the duty genuinely varies, the drive is the right answer and the impeller change is not. Where the duty is constant but the machine is too big, the drive is an expensive way to avoid the real correction.

## Power Factor, Harmonics and the Rest of the Installation

**Displacement power factor at a drive's input is typically high** — the rectifier and DC link mean the fundamental current is close to in phase with the voltage. This is frequently quoted as an advantage, and taken alone it is misleading.

**True power factor is P / S, and it includes distortion.** A drive drawing a heavily distorted current has an apparent power well above what the displacement factor suggests, so the true power factor is lower. Where a tariff or a utility limit is based on measured kVA or on true power factor, converting a plant to drives does not deliver the improvement the displacement figure implies.

**And the installation-level consequences are real:**

- Continuous harmonic current, whose thermal and resonance consequences are examined in their own right elsewhere in this series.
- Existing capacitor banks that were correct before the conversion and may resonate afterwards.
- Additional heat in electrical rooms, which is a cooling load and a derating factor for every device in them.

**These belong in the lifecycle calculation, not as footnotes.** A retrofit that saves energy at the machine and requires harmonic mitigation and additional cooling has a different economic case from the one in the original proposal.

## Bypass and Operational Trade-offs

**A bypass arrangement** — allowing the motor to run direct-on-line if the drive is unavailable — is worth considering where availability matters more than the last increment of saving. It also allows full-speed operation without drive losses on machines whose profile includes long periods at full output.

Other trade-offs that are decided at design time and felt in operation:

- **The control loop.** A throttled machine had a valve as its final element; a variable-speed machine has the drive. The loop must be retuned, and its behaviour at minimum speed and at the transition to a stopped state has to be defined.
- **Additional dependency.** Production now depends on a power-electronic device with a parameter set, a firmware version and a spares strategy.
- **Instrumentation.** Speed control needs a trustworthy process measurement; a drive controlled from a poor signal makes the process worse, not better.
- **Operator behaviour.** Where operators previously throttled by hand, the new control philosophy has to be understood and accepted, or the valve will simply be closed again with the drive at full speed — a result that is both energy-negative and hard to detect.

## Measurement and Verification

The difference between a claimed saving and a demonstrated one is a method agreed before the work starts.

**Establish a baseline first.** Measure the existing machine's electrical input against its process output — flow, pressure, production rate — over a representative period. A baseline taken during an atypical week produces a number that will be argued about for years.

**Agree the verification method in advance**, including:

- The measurement points and instruments, and who owns them.
- The normalising variable — production rate, ambient temperature, throughput, season — because comparing raw kilowatt-hours across a period when production changed proves nothing.
- The comparison period, long enough to cover the same variation as the baseline.
- What counts as success, stated as a range rather than a point.

**Measure the same thing after.** The most common verification failure is comparing a modelled "before" with a measured "after", or comparing a summer baseline with a winter result.

**Keep the profile.** The duty profile that justified the investment is also the reference for detecting later drift — a control setting changed, a valve reclosed, a minimum-speed limit raised — that quietly removes the saving.

## Failure Modes

**Cube law applied to a static-head system.** The model promises far more than the physics allows.

**Minimum-speed constraints discovered after installation.** The usable speed range is a fraction of the assumed one.

**Machine runs at full output most of the year.** Drive losses make the result negative.

**Oversized machine left oversized.** The drive compensates permanently for a one-time mechanical correction.

**Displacement power factor quoted as true power factor.** The tariff benefit does not materialise.

**Existing capacitors not reassessed.** Resonance appears months later.

**Electrical room cooling not recalculated.** Part of the saving is spent removing the drive's heat.

**Control loop not retuned.** Instability, or operators reverting to manual throttling with the drive at full speed.

**Baseline never measured.** The result cannot be demonstrated, only asserted.

**Verification against a different operating period.** The comparison is meaningless in either direction.

## A Representative Scenario

*The following is an illustrative engineering example, not an account of a specific project.*

A water utility converts a transfer pump station to variable speed. The business case is built on the cube law applied to the pumps' rated duty, assuming operation across a wide flow range, and projects a large annual saving.

After a year, metered consumption has fallen — by a fraction of the projection.

```text
Symptom:
Measured energy saving substantially below the projected figure.

Evidence:
- the station lifts water to a reservoir at a fixed elevation
- measured discharge pressure at reduced flow falls only slightly
- pump speed rarely goes below roughly three-quarters of rated
- below that speed, delivered flow collapses toward zero
- input power at reduced flow is well above the cube-law prediction
- drive input power at full speed is slightly above the previous
  direct-on-line figure for the same duty

Reasoning:
The system is static-head dominated. Most of the head is elevation,
which does not reduce with speed, so the machine must maintain nearly
full head across the flow range and the cube relationship does not
describe this installation. The narrow usable speed range is the same
physics seen from the other side: below the speed at which the pump
generates the static head, no flow is delivered at all. The full-speed
figure reflects the drive's own losses, which the original model omitted.

Next investigations:
- construct the actual system curve, separating static and friction head
- recompute the saving from the measured duty profile against that curve
- confirm minimum-flow and velocity constraints from process requirements
- assess whether pump selection or staging, rather than speed, matches the duty
```

Nothing failed. The drives work correctly, the pumps are healthy, and a real saving was achieved. What was wrong was the model: a relationship that describes a machine was applied to an installation whose system curve does not pass through the origin.

**The remediation is analytical rather than physical** — recompute against the real system curve, and consider whether staging two machines matches this duty better than modulating one. The transferable point is that the same drive on a friction-dominated system would have delivered close to the projection, and the difference between the two outcomes was decided before any equipment was purchased.

## Recommended Practice

- Measure the actual duty profile before modelling anything; log flow, pressure and electrical input over a representative period.
- Construct the system curve and separate static from friction head; apply the cube law only where static head is negligible.
- Establish minimum-speed constraints with the process engineer before assuming a speed range.
- Include drive losses, motor efficiency at reduced load, and additional room cooling as permanent subtractions from the benefit.
- Screen candidates by asking how far the throttling element is open, and for how much of the year.
- Compare against the alternatives — impeller trim, correct sizing, staging, removing an unnecessary pressure requirement — before specifying a drive.
- Use true power factor, not displacement factor, in any tariff or kVA argument.
- Reassess existing capacitor banks and electrical room cooling as part of the retrofit.
- Provide a bypass where availability matters or where long periods at full speed are expected.
- Retune the control loop and define behaviour at minimum speed and at stop.
- Agree the measurement and verification method, the normalising variable and the comparison period before the work starts.
- Keep the duty profile as a reference for detecting later drift.

## Conclusion

Variable-speed drives save energy by removing a waste that exists only in some installations. Where a machine is throttled hard for most of its life, the waste is large and the drive recovers most of it. Where the machine runs near full output, there is little to recover and the conversion adds loss.

The engineering that separates those two outcomes is not sophisticated: measure the duty profile, build the real system curve, ask the process for its minimum-speed limits, subtract the losses the conversion adds, and agree in advance how the result will be verified. Projects that do that produce results that survive scrutiny. Projects that start from a percentage and a nameplate produce a number that has to be defended for years.
